# Reference: Finding the image idea

Getting to the image idea must stay small — one line of conversation with the
user, then the procedure below does the rest. It is a procedure and not a
description on purpose. "Cinematic", "intriguing", "striking" are averaging
instructions in exactly the way "retro" and "grainy" are: they say what the
picture should be like without giving any way to invent one, and an agent with
nothing to invent from falls back on the only material in front of it — the
topic. That is where empty conference rooms come from.

What replaces them is one mechanism — **tension**: two emotional registers that
do not belong together, forced into one frame. Everything below is that idea and
the ways of staging it.

## Backgrounds are mood, not illustration

**The picture does not depict what the slide says.** This is the fix for the
literal-minded failure: a brand built on dossiers and files does *not* get
backgrounds of dossiers and files. The image carries atmosphere; the words carry
meaning.

In prompt terms: the subject of the words never enters the scene slot as a noun.
What enters is a physical situation with a temperature — light coming through a
dusty window, wet asphalt under a streetlamp, chalk haze in a low room, a hand
resting on a worn table. The mood answers the words; the picture doesn't repeat
them.

Test before generating: read the scene slot without knowing the caption. If you
can guess the caption from it, it is illustrating — replace the noun with a
situation.

A situation on its own is atmosphere, though, and atmosphere is not yet a
picture worth stopping on. What makes it one is the next section: the situation
carries one register, and something in the frame has to carry the register that
does not belong with it.

## Which images get a picture, and which kind

Density is governed by what the image is being asked to do, not by where it sits
in the set:

| Ask | Treatment |
|---|---|
| Feel or believe something | Constructed image — the recipe below |
| See or compare something | Literal demonstration — the thing itself, shown |
| Memorise a structure | No picture — flat ground, cards, or a diagram |

**The literal carve-out matters as much as the anti-illustration rule.** When
the claim is a claim about what an image should look like — a slide teaching
scale, or light, or one subject per frame — the image *is* the proof, and a
constructed surreal picture there destroys the lesson. Same when the artefact
under discussion is the deliverable itself: the real thing, shown, is the
illustration. Do not apply "the picture never depicts the topic" to these two
cases.

A set going flat later on is not fatigue. A set that argues a thesis keeps
constructed images all the way through; one that teaches a craft goes literal
from the second image. The variable is the ask, not the slide number.

Two jobs, kept apart: **this table decides whether an image gets a picture and
which mode it is in. The collision principle below decides what that picture
is.** Both run on every image in the set — the first one and all the others.
Interior images are constructed by the same method as the cover.

## The principle: two registers that collide

Every constructed image that works is two emotional registers that do not belong
together, held in one frame. That collision is the mechanism — it is what makes
a picture worth looking at when it has nothing to do with the topic.

- An astronaut — technological, sterile, isolated — kneeling in a field of
  flowers — organic, warm, abundant — performing tenderness.
- A head buried to the chin in a dune: human against geological.
- A boulder where a man's head should be: flesh against stone.
- A supercar parked alone in a wet empty moor: luxury against desolation.
- A black horse trotting in fog: alive against blank.

So the move is: **name two emotional registers that do not belong together, then
find the single image where they collide.** One of them is the feeling the words
need. The other is chosen for not belonging with it.

The seven operations are seven ways of staging that collision, not seven
unrelated tricks. When none of them fits what is in front of you, the principle
still holds — reason from the collision and build the frame yourself. An
operation is a shortcut to a collision, never a substitute for one.

## The recipe

Steps 2, 3 and 4 are the ones that do the work — they are what stops an agent
producing an empty conference room.

1. **Write the headline first.** The image serves the headline, not the topic.
   Every image in the set is composed around a specific line of type, not around
   a subject area.
2. **Name the feeling in one word, then name what it collides with.**
   Reverence, exposure, futility, solitude, suspense, menace, patience — that is
   the register the words need. The second register is chosen for not belonging
   with the first: sterile against organic, flesh against stone, luxury against
   desolation, alive against blank. The picture will carry those two and nothing
   else.
3. **Build the ban list — the critical step.** Write down every noun a reader
   would expect from the topic. Conference → room, stage, badge, lanyard,
   audience, podium, microphone, seats, slides, name tag. **None of these may
   appear in the image.** The topic supplies the feeling and one word; it never
   supplies the objects. Skipping this step is what produces empty conference
   rooms.
4. **Generate 5 candidates from 5 different operations** (below) — five ways of
   staging the same collision. Do not stop at the first. Then pick the candidate
   that is *furthest from the topic* while still carrying the two registers from
   step 2.
   - Register-match is the default.
   - If the headline contains a physical verb or a body word (*dips*, *buried*,
     *heavy*, *re-won*), try idiom-literalisation.
   - If the subject is an abstract entity that recurs (a model, a method, an
     archetype), cast it as a character.
   - If the headline warns against something, stage the thing it warns against,
     failing.
5. **One figure. Never two.** One thing to look at. Two competing focal points
   cancel each other out.
6. **Hide the face, and let the scene justify it.** Helmet, hood, silhouette,
   back turned, closed eyes, distance, mask, replaced by an object, or an animal
   instead of a person. Never a stranger's readable face. The one exception is
   self-as-subject, below.
7. **Displace it into a landscape that cannot contain the topic.** Orange
   desert, flower field, moor, foggy grass, mountainside at blue hour, cave
   mouth, empty stage, seamless studio grey. The landscape is usually where the
   second register lives — the wrongness of the world *is* the intrigue.
8. **Give it exactly one gesture, held.** Kneeling and cradling; crouching and
   reading; mid-leap; walking away; rappelling; arms out mid-turn; head bowed.
   One verb, frozen. No scenes with a sequence of actions.
9. **Set the scale and the ground.** Figure ≈ ¼–⅓ of frame height, standing on
   the lower third, horizon at or below the vertical midpoint. Or invert one
   object's scale instead: a planet held in the hands, a boulder where a head
   should be, a TV alone in a desert.
10. **One hard light source, low and behind.** Sun in frame is allowed. Result:
    rim-light, silhouette, long shadow toward camera.
11. **Grade the whole frame to one hue.** Which hue is a `design.md` question,
    not a picture question — what belongs here is the reasoning: the warm end of
    the palette for heat, ambition, urgency, anything that costs money or
    effort; the deep complement as the sky when there is one; full monochrome
    when the piece is about the plain, the cheap, or the mechanical.
12. **Reserve negative space before generating, not after.** Empty top third for
    the headline, empty bottom quarter for the kicker. Composition and type are
    designed together. This is the same low-ink zone `prompt-craft.md` requires
    under live text, decided at the idea stage instead of patched afterwards.
13. **Reject-check.** Does the image contain a banned noun from step 3? Does it
    still hold both registers, or has it collapsed into one? Would a stranger
    read it as an illustration *of the topic*? If it fails any of the three —
    discard, return to step 4. The correct answer to *"what does this picture
    have to do with the post?"* is *"nothing, until you read the headline."*

State the reject-check as output, before generating anything: the scene slot as
written, the banned nouns it contains (none is the only passing answer), the two
registers it holds, and what a stranger would guess the caption to be. An
unstated check is one nobody can see was skipped.

## The operations

Seven ways of staging the collision. Pick five different ones per image and
compare the results — that comparison is the whole point of the count.

| # | Operation | The collision it stages | Looks like |
|---|---|---|---|
| A | **Register-match displacement** | The feeling against a world that has no business containing it. Build a scene that *is* the word from step 2, in a world unrelated to the topic. The default move. | An astronaut — technological, sterile, isolated — kneeling in a field of orange flowers, cradling the Earth · a lone hiker on a golden hillside at blue hour · a hooded figure dancing alone under one spotlight |
| B | **Idiom literalisation** | Body against material. Take one physical word from the headline and build it as a real object — the nearest thing to a visual pun. | A boulder where a man's head should be, at a CRT: flesh against stone (*heavy head*) · a climber rappelling into a slot (*dips*) · one tomato balanced on a television (*the verdict*) |
| C | **Character casting** | Abstraction against animal, or against archetype. Give an entity a body whose temperament matches its role. | The fast, plain, tireless one as a black horse trotting in fog: alive against blank · the everyday one as a figure walking steadily into the daily sun · the big loud new one as a masked hero walking out of a fireball |
| D | **Argument-by-object** | The thing against the place that refutes it. Stage what the headline warns against, where it fails, and the image makes the argument. | *Stop impressing* → a white supercar parked alone in a wet empty moor: luxury against desolation, and nobody there to impress |
| E | **Artefact in the wrong world** | The real, printed, physical deliverable against a fabricated landscape. For when the post is "steal my X". | A figure crouching in a cracked orange desert with printed magazines laid out on the ground · covers floating blurred in hillside grass |
| F | **Scale inversion** | Size itself is the collision — one object at flagrantly the wrong size, held or worn like an ordinary one. Usually layered on top of A or B. | The Earth cradled like an infant · a boulder as a head · a television as the sole inhabitant of a desert |
| G | **Pure incongruity** | No collision — two registers that merely co-exist. The failure mode, named so it can be recognised and rejected. A head buried to the chin in a dune is human against geological, but under a headline about a storytelling formula it carries nothing, and no bridge can be constructed for it even in hindsight. Strangeness is not tension. | — |

## The constants

These hold across the constructed images in a set unless a row says otherwise.

| Property | Value | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Figure count | Exactly 1 | Two competing focal points cancel each other out |
| Face | Hidden or neutralised | Helmet, hood, silhouette, back turned, eyes closed, replaced by an object, mask, or an animal. Only exception: self-as-subject |
| Camera distance | Full-body / wide | Subject ≈ ¼–⅓ of frame height. Never a close crop of a stranger's face |
| Subject placement | Lower third, near-centred | Slight offset tolerated; never at an edge |
| Horizon | At or below the vertical midpoint | Absent on studio-seamless frames, which use a flat ground plane instead |
| Lighting | One hard source, low, behind the subject | Sun frequently in frame. Produces rim-light and a shadow running toward camera |
| Grade | One dominant hue across the whole frame | Values from `design.md`; full monochrome when the topic is plain, cheap, or mechanical |
| Palette count | 2 max | One hue plus its complement, or a single greyscale ramp. Never a third |
| Realism | Composited or constructed, never a shootable scene | A cracked desert with magazines in it, a boulder head, a TV in a desert, a supercar in a moor |
| Negative space | Designed in, top and bottom | Empty above for the headline, ground or shadow below for the kicker |
| Type vs subject | Type never crosses the face; it may deliberately interleave with the body | A helmet overlapping a word, a climber hanging between two words. Deliberate collision, not accident — and the collision is set live over the image, never generated into it |

## Degree, not method: the first image and the ones after it

Construction by collision is the rule for every image whose ask is *feel or
believe something* — interior images included. A set that argues a thesis is
constructed the whole way through, and the images after the first are not
decoration waiting for a topic.

What is special about the first image is degree. It carries the least obligation
to the topic of any image in the set, so it takes the strongest collision — a
harder pair of registers, a stronger situation, more depth, more drama.

It is not a different look. It uses the same STYLE and WORLD blocks as
everything after it, and the hook lives in the scene slot. A first image in its
own style reads as two different pieces stapled together.

## Self-as-subject, asked once, up front

If they want to be in it, that opens treatments — a period version, an
age-shifted version, a stylised version. Ask once, up front, because it changes
every prompt after it. Default is no.

This is the one permitted unhidden face, and it holds only while the piece is
about their own body of work. Everyone else stays helmeted, hooded, silhouetted
or turned away — step 6 has no other exception.

When the answer is yes, the person becomes a recurring element: one canonical
30–50 word description in the WORLD BLOCK, hexes included, reused verbatim in
every prompt where they appear. That is the general machinery for any recurring
element — see Procedure 3 in `prompt-craft.md`. Consistency comes from verbatim
repetition, not from hoping the model remembers.

## What this never asks

- Which layout, which type size, where things sit — that belongs to whatever
  renders the surface, and to the constraints, not to the person being asked
- Permission to proceed between steps that cost nothing
- The same thing twice in different words

If a third question wants asking, something upstream should have defaulted.

# Voice — how the sentences behave

Sequence rules live in `structures.md`. This file is about the line itself: what it may
say, how it is cut, and how it is set.

## Where the words come from

Pull them from the piece. Cut, shorten, drop, reorder — but never introduce a claim the
piece does not make.

This is the difference between a carousel and a rewrite. The author already decided what
they think; a slide that says something slightly better than the piece did says something
the author did not say. When a line will not fit, cut it down to what fits or drop it —
do not compose a replacement.

**No fabrication.** No invented statistics, no invented before/after, no case study that
did not happen, no "industry standard" claim with nothing behind it. If a number appears
on a slide, it appears in the piece. If the piece has no proof, the proof beat says so or
gets dropped — a stated gap is more useful than a plausible-sounding invention. Test: if
someone challenged this line, could you point at exactly where in the piece it came from?

## The voice rules

Short declaratives. No hedging, no "I think". Periods as rhythm. Numbers in headlines.
Asserts, then proves — never explains before showing. Second person. "Zero design skills
needed."

The asset giveaway is the content strategy: give away the means of production, sell the
depth.

## The tests, applied to a line

| Test | What it catches |
|------|-----------------|
| Read it with no context at all | A line that needs the slide before it. Every slide has to survive being screenshotted alone |
| Count the words on the cover | Over eight and they are already gone |
| "Do they feel caught?" | A mirror line that describes the audience instead of catching them |
| "Here's what I learned" → "so you can ___" | The turn written for the author instead of the reader. Mechanical rewrite, apply it literally |
| Cover the slide — does the next still make sense? | A broken spine. If it makes sense without the slide, the slide is not load-bearing |
| Point at the claim's source in the piece | Fabrication |

## What never appears on a slide

- Hedging: "I think", "arguably", "it could be said", "in my opinion".
- Throat-clearing: "In this carousel", "Let's dive in", "Here's the thing", "Buckle up".
- A slide that announces the next slide instead of earning it ("Keep swiping to find out").
- Emoji as punctuation, and em-dash-heavy connective prose. Slides are declaratives, not
  paragraphs.
- Credit lines, source attributions, "inspired by", or a method's provenance. The slides
  carry the user's brand and nothing else.
- Anything the author did not say.

## How the words are set

One idea per slide, max two supporting sentences. Never a paragraph — 2–4 statements,
each 1–2 lines, separated by hairline rules or generous space. Only the operative clause
is bolded.

**Emphasis falls on one or two words per headline, never more.** Set them in the display
face's italic where it has one: "how *many* times have you *heard* this?", "It *doesn't*
just add dots on top." The shape of the move is a mid-sentence tilt into emphasis, not a
whole emphasised phrase — three italic words in one headline is a sentence shouting, and
reads as decoration.

Casing comes from `design.md` (`case: sentence | upper | title`). Do not re-case an
approved string to suit a layout; a string approved at Gate 1 goes in as it was approved.
Casing is a property of the type role, applied by the composer — if a headline has to be
upper, it is upper for every headline in the set, not for the one that looked better.

## The caption

This skill does not write it. If the user wants one, say that plainly and offer the
slides. The caption is a second post with its own architecture, and writing it here would
put words in the author's mouth that never passed Gate 1.
